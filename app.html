Victorian rental application rules
From 31 March 2026, the application itself is prescribed. Rental providers and agents must use the prescribed rental application form and can only ask for information set out in that form. Personal information collected for the application can only be used to assess whether you are a suitable renter or to comply with another requirement of the Residential Tenancies Act 1997. What they may ask you for

Personal details

The prescribed application can ask for your full name, date of birth, current address, phone number and email address.

Employment and income details

It can ask for your current or most recent employer, employer address, net weekly income and, if you are not currently employed, when your most recent employment ended. If you do not have regular employment income, you can provide other information showing your ability to pay rent.

Financial evidence — maximum two documents

An agent may request no more than two financial documents to confirm capacity to pay. The standard list includes current or recent payslips, a bank statement without transaction details, and Centrelink payment statements or letters. For irregular income, other evidence such as savings or assets may be relevant.

Identity evidence — maximum two documents

An agent may request no more than two identity documents from the prescribed list. Examples include a driver licence, proof-of-age card, Medicare card, Australian passport, foreign passport with Australian visa, birth certificate, citizenship certificate, tertiary student ID, Centrelink card, DVA card, a utility bill at your current address, or a government letter addressed to your current address.

Rental or residential history

They may ask for previous addresses and rental-provider contact details.

Personal referees

If personal referees are requested, the form can seek the referee's name, phone number, email and connection to you.

Preferred lease term and move-in date

You can state the rental term you want and your preferred move-in date.

Tenancy-database checks

An agent can use tenancy databases to check rental history, but if they do, they must tell you the names of the databases they will use.

What they cannot ask for

Whether you disputed or took legal action against a previous rental provider

Your rental application cannot ask whether you have taken legal action or had a dispute with a rental provider.

Your bond history

They cannot ask whether a previous bond was claimed or ask questions about your bond history as part of the application.

Your day-to-day bank transactions

A bank statement may be requested as financial evidence, but detailed daily transaction information is not required. You can remove transactions you want to keep private.

Extra questions that are not on the prescribed form

Since 31 March 2026, optional-looking questions added by an agent or application platform are still not allowed if they are not part of the prescribed application form.

Protected personal characteristics without explaining why

If they ask about a protected characteristic — for example disability, race or gender identity — they must give you the reason for requesting that information in writing.

Privacy and application platforms

Your information has a limited purpose

Application information can be used to decide whether you are a suitable renter or to comply with another Residential Tenancies Act requirement. It is not an open-ended permission to collect extra personal data.

No fee just to inspect

A rental provider or agent cannot charge you a fee simply to show you a property.

Third-party application fees are restricted

From 31 March 2026, third-party rental platforms are banned from charging renters fees for making a rental application or paying rent.

Holding deposits have refund rules

A provider or agent may ask for a holding deposit before an agreement is signed, but it must be refunded once both parties sign. If no agreement is signed within 14 days, it must be refunded by the next business day.

What discrimination means in a rental application

Official guidance ↗
The basic rule

A rental provider can choose the applicant they consider most suitable using lawful factors such as rental history and ability to pay. They cannot treat an applicant unfavourably because of a protected personal characteristic.

Generally lawful selection

  • Comparing applicants' rental histories.
  • Assessing whether an applicant can pay the advertised rent using the evidence permitted by the prescribed form.
  • Choosing between applicants for legitimate, non-discriminatory reasons.

Examples of unlawful discrimination

  • Refusing an application because of race, disability, religion, age or another protected characteristic.
  • Processing an application differently because of a protected characteristic.
  • Offering the property on worse terms because of disability or another protected characteristic.
  • Refusing accommodation because a person uses an assistance dog.

Protected characteristics

Victoria's Equal Opportunity Act protects characteristics including the following. A specific exception can sometimes apply, so RentReady treats this list as a warning flag rather than automatically deciding that discrimination has occurred.

What about being on Centrelink?

Source of income is not itself listed as a protected characteristic in the Equal Opportunity Act. An agent is allowed to assess capacity to pay, and a Centrelink statement or letter is specifically recognised as permitted financial evidence. However, treatment connected to a payment may overlap with another protected characteristic — for example disability or parental status — and the prescribed-form rules still limit what can be asked.

Children

Generally, an agent or rental provider cannot refuse to rent to someone because a child aged 16 or under will live there. Consumer Affairs Victoria lists limited exceptions where the property is the provider's main residence or its location/design makes it unsuitable or inappropriate for children.

Disability and assistance dogs

A renter must not be refused because of disability or because they use an assistance dog. Disability discrimination can also include offering different terms. Reasonable disability-related alterations are governed by additional rules once renting.

Sexual harassment

Sexual harassment in accommodation is unlawful. Accommodation providers also have a positive duty under the Equal Opportunity Act to take reasonable and proportionate steps to eliminate discrimination, sexual harassment and victimisation as far as possible.

Victimisation

It is unlawful to treat somebody badly because they made a discrimination, sexual-harassment or vilification complaint, or helped another person make one.

Pets are different from assistance dogs. Consumer Affairs Victoria says refusing an applicant because they have an ordinary pet is not, by itself, unlawful discrimination. Assistance dogs are treated differently because they are connected with disability protections.

If something in the application looks wrong

1
Ask what rule allows the question

If it is not on the prescribed form, ask the agent to identify why they are requesting it.

2
Ask for the reason in writing

If the question relates to a protected characteristic, the reason for asking must be given in writing.

3
Keep evidence

Save the listing, screenshots of the application form, emails, messages and any written reason given to you.

4
Use the appropriate complaint pathway

Consumer Affairs Victoria deals with rental-law compliance. The Victorian Equal Opportunity and Human Rights Commission can help with discrimination complaints, and VCAT can consider claims including compensation for discrimination.
